U2

Educational drone kit

A solder-free, modular drone kit that secondary-school pupils assemble and programme themselves. I proposed it for the society's schools outreach and designed its electronics.

Role
Proposed the kit; designed the electronics
Where
Glasgow University Drones, as Head of Electronics
When
2025 to 2026
Board
Two layers, 70.6 × 70.6 mm
Main parts
ESP32-C3 module, ICM-42670-P IMU, two DRV8833 dual H-bridges, TPS63001 buck-boost, BQ24074 charger
Status
First boards fabricated; assembly and testing next

Why a kit

The society runs outreach with secondary schools. I proposed a drone that pupils could build and program themselves without a soldering iron, and that could be taken apart and reused by the next group. It's the society's own project; the University isn't involved.

Design decisions

  • No soldering for pupils. JST connectors on the battery and motor outputs, and a USB-C receptacle for both power and programming.
  • A steady 3.3 V rail. A TPS63001 buck-boost converter holds 3.3 V across the LiPo battery's full discharge range. A diode-OR power path takes power from USB or the battery, with reverse-polarity protection, and a BQ24074 charges the battery.
  • Four motors from two chips. Two DRV8833 dual H-bridges drive the four motors.
  • Sensors that fit the pins. I ruled out sensors whose interfaces the microcontroller's free pins couldn't support, and presented the evidence to the team. The kit uses an ICM-42670-P IMU.
  • Safe for a classroom. Low-voltage rechargeable batteries and propeller guards, with the principles of the BS EN 71 toy-safety standard considered.
  • Fits the frame. I agreed the board's size and the position of parts, including the battery, with the mechanical team designing the chassis.

Running the project

The team compared candidate parts against fixed criteria: compatibility, communication protocol, reliability, function, footprint and cost. I put together a budget of about £500 with a contingency, which was approved, and compared quotes from several PCB manufacturers. Once the design passed ERC and DRC I released the Gerbers and handed ordering to the team.

As Head of Electronics I lead a team of three to five engineers, approve every schematic and PCB layout before it's released, and run KiCad workshops for members.
